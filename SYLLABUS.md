# Syllabus: Human-Computer Interaction

**BIL-429 İnsan-Bilgisayar Etkileşimi. Undergraduate elective course, Department of Computer Engineering, Süleyman Demirel University, Isparta, Türkiye**

Instructor: Prof. Dr. Utku Kose, Department of Computer Engineering, Süleyman Demirel University. Contact: utkukose@sdu.edu.tr, utkukose@gmail.com. This course is updated in line with current developments in the field. First version: November 2026.

## Course information

Course code: BIL-429. Type: Undergraduate elective course of the Department of Computer Engineering, Süleyman Demirel University. Language: English. Format: Fourteen weeks, one session of about three hours per week, usable in synchronous and asynchronous study.

## Course description

Human-computer interaction (HCI) studies how people use interactive computing systems and how such systems should be designed, evaluated and built for human use [1]. Over fourteen weeks, the course leads from the interaction loop, user-centred design and usability [2, 3, 4] to the abilities of the human. These are perception and the psychological effects of colour, attention, memory and movement [5]. It then covers design principles, user research, prototyping, visual design and the statistics of usability testing [6]. It continues with accessibility [7], mobile and multimodal interaction and three-dimensional (3D) user interfaces [8]. The last two weeks turn to interaction with artificial intelligence (AI): Trust, reliance, explanations, conversation, dark patterns and the ethics of design [9].

## Prerequisites

Basic programming in any language and first-year mathematics, in particular functions, logarithms and elementary probability. Python is taught from the first step; the optional warm-up in `start-here/` introduces Colab and the first Python steps.

## Learning outcomes

1. Explain the interaction loop, user-centred and system-centred design, usability and user experience, and measure usability with standard measures and questionnaires.
2. Relate interface design to perception, including the psychological effects of colour, and to attention, memory and movement, and predict performance with Fitts' law and the keystroke-level model.
3. Apply design principles and usability heuristics to evaluate interfaces and to rank usability problems.
4. Carry out user research, build personas and task analyses, and prototype interaction flows.
5. Plan and analyse usability tests with questionnaires, confidence intervals and controlled comparisons.
6. Design for accessibility and inclusion, and for mobile, multimodal and three-dimensional interaction.
7. Design and evaluate interaction with AI systems, including mental models, trust, reliance, explanations, conversation and ethics.
8. Write and run Python programs in Google Colab that simulate users, analyse interaction data and build small interactive prototypes.

## Learning activities and workload

Each week combines a lecture page with formulas, worked examples, an interface clinic, knowledge checks, an interactive scene, animations and review cards, also available as printable lecture notes. An interactive lab adds practice parts, usually including an experiment in three dimensions, a self-assessment and reflection prompts. A Colab notebook opens with the Python step of the week and continues with hands-on exercises on the clinic app. Application challenges, a weekly task and an optional research assignment complete the week. The overview page of each week lists them together with the study path. The expected workload is seven to ten hours per week, including the class session when the course is taught in person.

## Assessment

Assessment rests on two projects, each a coding application with a short technical report. The midterm project at the end of Week 7 studies an interface of the student's choice with the methods of Weeks 1 to 7. It combines a baseline model, a heuristic evaluation, personas, predicted task times and a redesign proposal. The final project at the end of Week 14 designs, prototypes and evaluates an interface with an AI feature, including a usability test, an accessibility audit and measures of reliance. The midterm component counts 40 percent of the course grade and the final project 60 percent. Each week also offers an optional task, an optional research and report assignment and a self-assessment in the interactive lab. The optional work carries no separate weight. It is combined with the midterm component, and the instructor may take it into account as a discretionary adjustment. During an active semester, the deadlines are announced by the instructor, and the optional work can be sent to utkukose@sdu.edu.tr or utkukose@gmail.com.

Reports use the Word template `exams/REPORT_TEMPLATE.docx`, cite their sources in square brackets and include the Colab notebooks that produced their results. Unless an assignment states otherwise, a research report has 1500 to 2500 words, follows the structure of an academic paper and cites at least six scholarly or official sources.

## Policy on generative AI tools

Generative AI tools may be used as assistants for explanation, debugging and drafting, under four conditions. Students state in every submission which tools were used and for what. Students verify every factual, numerical and code claim themselves and remain fully responsible for its correctness. Students cite the primary sources that support their work, not the tool. Finally, students do not enter confidential, personal or unpublished data into external services, which includes data of real users collected in usability studies. Submissions that present unverified generated content as the student's own analysis are treated as academic misconduct [10, 11].

## Academic integrity

Collaboration in discussing ideas is encouraged. Code, reports and reflections are individual unless a task states otherwise. Code taken from other sources, including the reference solutions of the Colab notebooks, must be acknowledged. Studies with people, even short usability tests with classmates, ask for consent and keep the data anonymous.

## Accessibility

The lecture pages and interactive labs are designed for keyboard use, legible fonts, sufficient contrast and reduced motion when the operating system requests it. The 3D experiments can be operated with the keyboard. Students who need other adjustments can contact the instructor.

## Main textbooks and open resources

Norman for the principles of design [2], Rogers, Sharp and Preece for interaction design [12], and MacKenzie for empirical research in HCI [13]. Card, Moran and Newell for the models of the human [5], and Sauro and Lewis for the statistics of usability testing [6]. LaViola and colleagues for 3D user interfaces [8], and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines for accessibility [7]. The guidelines of Amershi and colleagues and the work of Shneiderman frame the weeks on human-AI interaction [9, 14].
