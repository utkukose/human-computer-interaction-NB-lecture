Users and tasks lab

Three parts of this interactive lab are games in three dimensions (3D) that record your own actions. Part A asks you to interview patients in a waiting hall, and Part B to group them into personas. Part D lets you book appointments at the front desk with two methods. Parts A and D also repeat the formulas of the lecture step by step, and Part C computes the expected time of a task. Part E asks for the values of stage 6 by hand. In the calculators every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value. The results of Parts A, B and D can be copied into the Colab notebook. Each game first shows its room of the hospital. Start moves the view to the game, and the view returns to the room at the end. The games work best with a mouse or a touchpad and a keyboard on a computer.

Part A: Interviews in the waiting room

The game below takes place in the waiting hall of the outpatient clinics. Twelve patients wait for their appointments, and the team of the clinic app allows you eight interviews. Click a patient in the scene, then choose the question that opens the interview: An open question, a closed question or a leading question. The answer appears above the patient, and every need that you hear becomes a note on the affinity wall behind the chairs.

Choose the patients with care, because each patient has different needs and some needs are rare. A patient can be interviewed only once. At the end, the table lists the eight themes of the wall and how many interviews revealed each of them.

Part B: Personas from the waiting hall

The same twelve patients now stand in a hall with three zones on the floor. Each patient has a profile of four numbers: The age, the visits a year, the digital skill from 1 to 5 and the travel time to the clinic. Click a patient to see the profile, then click a zone or press 1, 2 or 3 to send the patient to that group. A patient can be moved again at any time.

Form three groups whose members are close to each other. After each move the page computes the within-group sum of squares \(W\) of the z-scores, as in the lecture. The board on the wall shows the mean of each group. A small \(W\) means tight groups. Finish when all twelve patients have a group.

Part D: The front desk race and the keystroke-level model

The game below puts you behind the reception desk as the nurse. Patients come with a request and a ticket, and you book their appointments in the booking software on the screen. The game has two blocks of three bookings, in an order drawn by the page. In one block you use the menus and the mouse. Open the menu of departments, choose the department and a free time and click into the field of the ticket. Then type its four digits and click Book. In the other block you use codes on the keyboard. Type the code of the department and press Enter, and press Enter again for the first free time. Then type the ticket and press Enter.

The codes stand on a yellow note on the screen. Each request appears at the bottom of the view and below the scene. A booking with a wrong department or a wrong ticket is refused and counts as an error. The page times every booking from the moment the request appears, and the table compares your mean time with the prediction of the keystroke-level model.

Part E: By hand: Stage 6 for Ayten

Ayten, the carer, needs 1.6 s for a mental preparation M and 0.7 s for a tap T. The current kiosk takes 6 M and 15 T plus 5.62 s for the menu and 0.80 s for the slide. The redesigned kiosk takes 4 M and 5 T plus 3.55 s for the menu and 2.0 s for the card. Compute her two predicted times to two decimals. Then compute the hours that the redesign would save in a year if all 600 × 250 bookings saved as much time as Ayten.
